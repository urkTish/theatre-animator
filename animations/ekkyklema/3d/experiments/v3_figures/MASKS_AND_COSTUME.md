# Masks and costume for the v3 figures (c. 425–400 BC)

Research behind `figures_v3.py`. Tags as in `../../../SOURCES.md`: **[A]** attested (an ancient text or image read by us), **[I]** inferred (scholarly inference), **[R]** our reconstruction choice. Check marks: ✔ read or looked at by us; ◐ known through another scholar's citation; ○ search snippet only.

## 1. The lead's hypothesis: confirmed, with two refinements

The hypothesis: 5th-century tragic masks were fairly naturalistic, with lips only slightly parted and hair attached to the mask; the gaping masks with the tall *onkos* are Hellenistic and Roman.

The evidence agrees:
- **Naturalistic, no onkos, no "expression of terror".** Roscino (2025), surveying the Attic vase evidence, says: "Wherever they can be perceived, the masks in the testimonies of this chronological phase … are always of a naturalistic type and lack those features – the high rise on the forehead (ónkos) and the expression of terror – which characterized them in the Hellenistic and Roman ages" (citing Pickard-Cambridge, *DFA*, It. ed. 1996, pp. 261–70). **[I]** ✔
- **The gaping mouth, wide-open eyes, onkos, padding and thick-soled boots** came with the Hellenistic raised stage: "the marked deformation of the masks with high ónkos … and wide-open eyes and mouth and bodies … burdened with padding and supported by thick-soled shoes" (Roscino 2025, citing Green 1994, pp. 105–71). The 19th-century picture of tragic costume was built on this later evidence. **[I]** ✔
- **On the Pronomos Vase** (Naples 81673, Attic, c. 410–400 BC) the actors hold their masks (looked at in the ArchaiOptix photograph, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0). **[A]** ✔
  - The king's mask, carried by its hair at the actor's side, is a whole head: face, curly dark hair and full beard in one piece.
  - It has painted eyes with irises, a level, calm brow and a **small** open mouth. Nothing about it is exaggerated.
  - A second bearded mask (in profile) has slightly parted lips and long hair.
- **Pollux, *Onomasticon* 4.133–141** (2nd c. AD), read in Bethe 1900, pp. 241–243 (archive.org OCR, decoded by us). ✔
  - He defines the onkos ("the part above the face rising to a height, Λ-shaped", 4.133) and gives it to most of his 28 masks.
  - His catalogue reflects later, systematised practice. Haigh (1907, p. 245) already noted that Pollux drew on Alexandrian sources. So he is **not** evidence for 425 BC masks. **[I]**
- **Conclusion:** the current mannequins' masks and the MakeHuman test's masks are anachronistic. Their wide open mouths, black eye holes and pure white faces are a Hellenistic–Roman (and 19th-century) image. The v3 masks follow the Pronomos Vase. **[I]**

### Refinement 1: "naturalistic" means idealised and calm, not a portrait

- Wiles (2007) argues the mask's "angles combined in an idealised neutral form" that could carry "multiple meanings" (◐ via Dunbar's review, *Didaskalia* 2007).
- Moncur's 2025 Open University MPhil thesis traces "the development of the form of the neutral tragic mask" through vase-paintings. It argues that the poets used verbal signposting to make audiences imagine changes of expression "on the blank surface of the neutral mask" (abstract, ✔ via the ORO OAI record; the PDF itself was behind a bot wall from here).
- So: a calm, slightly grave expression, with no smile and no scream. **[I]**

### Refinement 2: eyes

- Vase-painters draw the masks with seeing eyes (✔ Pronomos). A real mask needs openings.
- Haigh (1907, p. 244) says the white of the eye was painted and the pupil left hollow. His source is Wieseler's *Denkmäler*, which covers later monuments (◐).
- We paint the eye white and iris, with a small dark pupil opening. That reads as eyes at a distance, not as black holes. **[I]/[R]**

### Skin colour: men darker, women pale

| Evidence | What it shows | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| Pronomos Vase | The female mask's face is in added white, while the male masks are left in the clay colour (✔ looked at) | **[A]** for c. 400 BC |
| Ar. *Ecclesiazusae* 63–64 (391 BC) | A woman oils herself and stands in the sun all day to tan, so she can pass as a man (✔ Perseus Greek text) | **[A]** for the norm |
| Ar. *Thesm.* 191–192 (411 BC) | Agathon, effeminate, is *leukos* ("pale"), shaven, woman-voiced (✔) | **[A]** |
| Pollux 4.133–140 | Grades complexions: men *melas* (dark), *euchrous* (healthy), *hypōchros* (sallow, for the sick); women mostly *ōchra*/*hypōchros* (pale) or *leukē* (white) (✔ OCR) | **[A]** for Pollux's time |

**Our choice:** the male masks are painted in a warm tanned ochre-brown, not white. **[R]**, from **[A]**.

### Euripides' mask
- **Hair and beard:** Euripides as a stage character says "I am grey-haired and have a beard" (*Thesm.* 190, 411 BC, ✔). So the mask has grizzled hair and a full grizzled beard, cf. Pollux's *spartopolios*, "sprinkled-grey" (4.133–134). **[A]**→**[R]**
- **The standing actor:** a man in his prime with dark hair and beard (cf. Pollux's *melas*). **[R]**

### Material and form
- **Material:** linen, occasionally cork or wood. Sources: Suda s.v. Thespis (linen masks); Pollux 10.167; Isidore (◐ all via Haigh 1907, p. 244 n. 729). **[I]**
- **Finish [R]:** linen stiffened with a plaster or gesso ground, painted with matt mineral pigments (ochre, carbon black, red ochre/cinnabar for lips, white). This is the usual modern reconstruction.
- **Whole head [I]:** the mask covered the whole head, with the hair (wig) attached. Gellius 5.7 says so for Roman masks ("as in the Greek", ◐ via Haigh and Roscino). The Pronomos masks are whole heads held by their hair (✔).
- **Beard:** part of the mask. The Pronomos masks are bearded, while the actors themselves are clean-shaven or lightly bearded (✔).

## 2. Costume

### Actors: long-sleeved, patterned, ankle-length robe, with a mantle
- **The *poikilon*.** The tragic chiton is "a chiton decorated with various ornamental motifs" (Pollux 4.115–118, via Roscino 2025). **[A]** for Pollux; **[I]** for the 5th century.
- **The Pronomos king** (✔ looked at). **[A]** for c. 400 BC
  - An ankle-length robe with **long sleeves to the wrist**.
  - It is covered in horizontal bands: running spirals or waves, rows of palmettes, figure friezes (warriors, animals) and rows of dots, with a belt.
  - A mantle is draped over it.
  - Roscino (2025) cites Wyles 2010, pp. 248–52 (the "refined and colorful processing of the fabrics", "typical of the tragic poikílon") and Roscino 2006, pp. 104–11 (the long sleeves). Pronomos the piper wears a similar sleeved, decorated dress.
- **Caution on the date.**
  - Sleeves and trousers with showy decoration "may initially have involved only characters of non-Greek ethnicity" (Corinth chorus fragments, c. 470–460; the Andromeda vases, 2nd half of the 5th century).
  - Richer, sleeved costume for the main characters spreads "at the end of the 5th century BC" with the professional actors (Roscino 2025).
  - So long-sleeved robes on Greek characters in 425 BC extrapolate the c. 400 BC evidence back by about 15–25 years. **[I]** We follow the brief: sleeves for both actors. **[R]**
- **Mantle (himation):** over the left shoulder, with the right arm free, as in ordinary dress. **[I]** Its colour and border are **[R]**.
- **Boots:**
  - The two principal actors on the Pronomos Vase wear high-fitting laced boots, "similar in shape to the embádes" (Roscino 2025). **[A]** for c. 400
  - The thick-soled *kothornos* belongs to Roman practice (Roscino 2025). Wyles even doubts kothornoi for the vase's Heracles (BMCR 2012.03.17, ✔).
  - **Our choice:** soft, low-soled, calf-high leather boots. **[R]**
- **Dyes [R]:** plant and insect dyes the Greeks had.
  - Madder red, woad blue, weld or saffron yellow, undyed cream, and dark brown-black (iron-tannin).
  - Haigh (1907, p. 250, from Pollux 4.116–118 ◐) lists tragic mantles of saffron, "frog-green", gold and purple.
  - We keep the scene's palette (saffron, madder, woad, undyed) and add pattern bands in madder, woad and dark brown.

### Euripides in *Acharnians*: "rags from tragedy"
- At *Ach.* 412–413 Dikaiopolis asks, "But why do you have the rags from tragedy on, a pitiful garment?" (*τὰ ῥάκι' ἐκ τραγῳδίας … ἐσθῆτ' ἐλεινήν*, ✔ Perseus). **[A]**
- **Our choice:** a long-sleeved saffron robe with a patterned border, a little faded. **[R]**

### Stagehands: plain short tunic
- Nothing is attested about stagehands' dress.
- **Workmen's and slaves' wear** in Attic art and comedy is the short *chitoniskos* or the *exomis*, "with the only left brace for manual workers and slaves" (Roscino 2025). Pollux 4.118 describes the exomis as "a white chiton, plain, without a seam on the left side, sleeveless" (✔ OCR). **[A]** for workmen's dress
- **Our choice:** knee-length tunics of undyed or dun coarse wool, belted, barefoot. **[R]**

## 3. A caveat for the lead: *Acharnians* is a comedy

- In *Acharnians* (425 BC), Euripides and Dikaiopolis are characters in an **Old Comedy**, played by comic actors.
- Comic costume was a padded body suit (*somation*), a short chiton, a phallus and a grotesque mask (Roscino 2025, comedy section; Green 2002). **[I]** For a real person like Euripides, the mask may have been a caricature portrait (cf. *Knights* 230–233 on the mask-makers' fear of Cleon, ◐).
- Our figures are **tragic** in style. This is the device's home genre, and the reveals in beat 8 are all tragic. The comedy scene parodies a tragic ekkyklema reveal, and its Euripides is literally dressed in "rags from tragedy".
- So the figures fit "a tragic tableau on the ekkyklema". If a caption names *Acharnians*, the costume is an **[R]** simplification. A caption like "as parodied in *Acharnians*" would make it honest.
