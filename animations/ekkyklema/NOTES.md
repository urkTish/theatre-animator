## Research questions

1. **Ancient evidence.** What do the ancient texts actually say (Pollux, Aristophanes, scholia)?
2. **Mechanism.** Was it rolled out on wheels or rollers, or did it revolve or pivot (the turntable theory, the *exostra*)?
3. **Size.** How wide was the skene door, and so how big could the platform be? How many figures could it carry?
4. **Typical use.** Which scenes in surviving plays use it? Which are agreed and which disputed?
5. **First use.** Was it in use by the *Oresteia* (458 BC), or later?
6. **Setting.** In the 5th century: one door or three? A raised stage? A wooden skene?
7. **Movement.** Who moved it and how (stagehands pushing, rails, grooves)? Any archaeological traces?
8. **Visual evidence.** Any vase paintings or reliefs? Reliable modern reconstruction drawings?

## Verified primary sources

### Pollux, *Onomasticon* 4.128
Read in Bethe's Teubner edition (1900), vol. 1, p. 239: https://archive.org/details/pollucisonomasti01polluoft/page/239

- A platform on wooden supports. The word "high" appears in only part of the manuscript tradition, so the animation should not stress height.
- There is a seat (thronos) on it.
- It shows deeds done in secret inside the houses behind the skene.
- The verb for using it is *ekkyklein* ("to roll out").
- The device for bringing it back in is called the *eiskyklema*.
- There is one at each door, that is, one for each house.
- Caveat: Pollux is 2nd century AD, about 600 years after the classical plays. He may describe later theatres.

### Aristophanes, *Acharnians* 393–480 (425 BC)
Greek text: Hall & Geldart (Oxford), from the PerseusDL canonical-greekLit repository on GitHub (`tlg0019.tlg001.perseus-grc2`). Perseus itself returned 503 errors.

- 395–403: Dikaiopolis knocks at Euripides' door. The servant (Kephisophon) says Euripides is "inside and not inside". He is "writing a tragedy with his feet up" (*anabadēn*, 399).
- **408**: Dikaiopolis: *all' ekkyklēthēt'* ("well, get rolled out!"). Euripides: "Impossible."
- **409**: Euripides: *all' ekkyklēsomai; katabainein d' ou scholē* ("All right, I'll be rolled out, but I have no time to come down"). So he stays on the platform, raised up (on a couch?), and does not step onto the ground.
- 410–411: joke: "you write with your feet up when you could write with them down; no wonder you create lame heroes."
- 432–434: Euripides tells the servant to fetch Telephus' rags, which "lie above Thyestes' rags, between Ino's". So the costume store is inside, offstage. The servant comes and goes while Euripides stays on the platform.
- **479**: Euripides: *klēie pēkta dōmatōn* ("shut the house's fastened doors"). This ends the scene. No explicit "roll me back in" verb here; going back in is implied by the doors shutting.
- Why it matters: this is the earliest secure use of the word. It is a parody, so the audience must have known the device from tragedy (Euripides especially) by 425 BC.

### Aristophanes, *Thesmophoriazusae* 95–265 (411 BC)
Same edition (`tlg0019.tlg008.perseus-grc2`).

- 95: Euripides: "Quiet! Agathon is coming out."
- **96**: "There he is, the one being rolled out" (*houkkykloumenos*). The poet Agathon appears on the platform, mid-composition, and sings (99ff.).
- The platform carries props: female clothes and a little couch (*klinis*, 261). Euripides and his kinsman take a garment "from the couch" to dress the kinsman as a woman.
- **265**: Agathon: *eisō tis hōs tachist' m' eskyklēsatō* ("Someone roll me back inside, quickly!"). This is the verb that matches Pollux's *eiskyklema*.
- Caution: *enkyklon* at 261 is a garment (a round wrap), not the machine.
- Why it matters: it confirms rolling out (*ekkyklein*) and rolling back in (*eiskyklein*) as two named operations, done by unseen helpers ("someone").

### Sophocles, *Ajax* (for animation 2)
Greek text: Storr's Loeb (1913), PerseusDL (`tlg0011.tlg003.perseus-grc2`).

- 344: Chorus: *all' anoigete* ("open up!").
- 346–347: Tecmessa: *idou, dioigō* ("look, I am opening it"). "You may look at his deeds, and at the state he is in."
- 348ff.: Ajax laments, revealed among the slaughtered animals.
- **579**: Ajax: *kai dōma paktou* ("and shut the house").
- **581**: *pykaze thasson* ("close it up, quickly").
- **593**: *ou xunerxeth' hōs tachos?* ("will you not shut me in, quickly?").
- The word *ekkyklema* is not in the text. Its use here is inferred from the opened doors and the revealed interior.
